# Negative space and tension

*Read when: diagnosing "too much whitespace", "it feels cramped", or a layout that
is balanced and lifeless. Supports [C4](../SKILL.md#invariant) and
[C10](../SKILL.md#invariant).*

---

## Space has a shape, and the shape is a decision

The area between two masses is a form with proportion, direction and pressure.
Nobody has to design it for it to exist — the only question is whether it was
designed.

Name the shapes. A usable description: *"a tall narrow channel between the type
mass and the image, roughly 1:6, which reads as pressure"* or *"a wide shallow
band beneath the opening, about the height of the headline, which reads as a
breath before the evidence."* If the space cannot be described this way, it is
residue.

## Six kinds of empty, and only two of them are the same thing

| Kind | What it does | How to recognise it |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional spacing** | Separates for legibility | Small, systematic, token-driven |
| **Compositional interval** | Sets rhythm and pace | Large, deliberate, varies between passages |
| **Atmospheric space** | Establishes register and isolation | Surrounds the subject; makes it monumental or intimate |
| **Semantic separation** | Says "these are different things" | Sits exactly where a group boundary is |
| **Trapped space** | Nothing — a pocket the layout could not resolve | Awkward proportion, no relationship to neighbours |
| **Unresolved emptiness** | Reads as unfinished | Large, shapeless, at the end or edge of a composition |

The first four are working. The last two are the ones people mean when they say a
layout "feels off", and they are fixed by **changing the masses**, not by filling
the hole.

## Do not fill space because it is empty

The reflex to fill is the single most reliable way to destroy a composition. Before
adding anything to an empty region, ask:

1. Is it isolating the subject? Then it is the reason the subject reads.
2. Is it counterweighting a mass? Then filling it will unbalance the page.
3. Is it a pause before something? Then filling it deletes the pacing.
4. Is it trapped or unresolved? **Then the fix is upstream** — the masses are
   wrong, the interval is wrong, or the format is being used at a proportion it
   cannot carry. Adding an element hides the symptom.

Equally: **"generous whitespace" is not a quality signal.** A large empty field
with no shape and no function is not restraint; it is an unmade decision at scale.
Do not call it luxury.

## Density is a legitimate answer

Air is a dialect preference ([spacing D1](../../spacing-taste/SKILL.md#dialect)),
not a law. Trading floors, dashboards, timetables, catalogues and reference tools
are correctly dense: the operator's task is comparison, and comparison requires
proximity. In a dense composition, figure-ground is *harder*, so it needs more
attention rather than less — the separations do the work that space would do,
using value, rule, and alignment instead.

## Tension

Tension is force that has not resolved. It is not a defect; it is what makes a
composition hold attention. The defect is tension that is **unintended** or
**uncontained**.

Sources, in interface work:

- **Edge tension** — a mass close to the frame without touching it. The gap
  becomes charged. Very small gaps read as an error; commit or clear.
- **Near-contact** — two masses almost touching. The most uncomfortable
  relationship available, and occasionally exactly what is wanted.
- **Scale tension** — masses at ~1:1.2, close enough to compare, different enough
  to rank.
- **Directional tension** — vectors opposing each other.
- **Density contrast** — a packed region against an open one.
- **Asymmetric pressure** — weight loaded to one side, held by something.
- **Interruption** — a break in an established rhythm.
- **Delayed resolution** — the eye is led toward something that arrives later,
  after a pause.

## Counterweight and containment

Every deliberate tension needs one of:

- **Counterweight** — an opposing mass, or a field of space, that holds it.
- **Containment** — the frame itself, or an enclosing form, holding the force in.
- **Continuation** — the force carries into the next passage and resolves there.
- **Release** — the following passage lets go: quieter, opener, slower.

**And one legitimate fourth option: nothing.** Some compositions depend on force
that never resolves — a portfolio that stays uneasy, a campaign whose whole point
is that it does not settle. That is a decision, and it is stated in the Read.
Unstated, it is indistinguishable from an accident, and readers will assume the
accident.

## When tension becomes disorder

The threshold: **can the viewer tell the tension is intentional?**

Signals that it reads as intentional — the tension is singular (one unresolved
force, not five); something in the composition is stable enough to measure the
disruption against; the tension coincides with meaning rather than sitting on an
arbitrary element; and near-misses are absent (nothing 3px out of alignment).

Signals that it reads as a mistake — several forces unresolved at once; nothing
stable; alignment "almost" happening; and the tension landing on a low-importance
element, which reads as carelessness rather than emphasis.

## Failure signatures

| Observation | Cause | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| "Too much whitespace" | Space without shape or function | Shape it — change the mass proportions, not the amount |
| "Cramped" | No interval hierarchy; every gap the same | Open the between-passage gaps only |
| "Awkward gap I can't fix" | Trapped space from an unresolvable mass ratio | Change one mass's size or alignment |
| "Balanced but lifeless" | No tension anywhere | Introduce one: scale, direction, or asymmetric weight |
| "Uncomfortable and I don't know why" | Near-contact or a near-alignment | Commit or clear |
| "Exhausting" | Multiple unresolved tensions | Resolve all but one |
